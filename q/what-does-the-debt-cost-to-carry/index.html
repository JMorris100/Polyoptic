What does UK government debt cost to carry?

4.3% of GDP in 2025: the interest bill is up from 2.7% in 1993, a much smaller rise than the debt’s, which went from 29.7% to 94.2% of GDP.

Source: ONS Public Sector Finances · Open Government Licence v3.0

The claim

Government debt is at a record high, so debt interest must be too.

The numbers

In 1993 net debt was 29.7% of GDP and the interest on it cost 2.7% of GDP. By 2025 the debt was 94.2% of GDP, 3.2 times its 1993 share of the economy, but interest cost 4.3% — 1.6 times, and it peaked at 4.7% in 2022. The difference is the rate: interest ÷ debt, the effective rate the state pays, went from 8.9% in 1993 to a low of 2.1% in 2020, and is back to 4.6% in 2025. Interest and debt are both set against the size of the economy here, so rising prices and growth do not flatter or inflate either one.

What it actually shows

Two different kinds of number, made comparable. Debt is a stock measured at a point in time; interest is a flow paid across a year, so in cash the two cannot be set side by side. Against GDP, the interest bill is what weighs on a budget; against the debt, it is the price of borrowing.

A bigger debt costs more to carry only if that price holds, and when rates move the bill follows with a lag, as old borrowing is refinanced.

Why a line chart

Both lines are percentages: interest as a share of the economy, and as a share of the debt. On one axis from zero, the space between them is the price of borrowing.

The indicators

Read it carefully

Both series exclude public sector banks. Debt interest is interest and dividends paid to the private sector and the rest of the world, and a large share of it in recent years reflects RPI uplift on index-linked gilts rather than coupon payments. The effective rate is approximate: it divides interest paid across a year by the debt at the end of it.
